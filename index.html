Australia's fossil collections

Written in Stone

Two hundred years of fossil hunting in Australia have uncovered over three billion years of life on Earth. Where were these fossils found, how old are they and who looks after them today?

By Ian Zheng Yan Leong  |  Monash University Malaysia  |  Published 15 October 2026

Australia is about 23 times the size of Malaysia and its rocks hold one of the longest fossil records on the planet. Scientists have described fossils from every state, from tiny shells to giant marsupials and dinosaur footprints.

This page follows those fossils through four questions: where they come from, how old they are, what kinds of life they show and how well this shared natural heritage is protected and kept.

Three words used on this page

Fossil

The remains or traces of ancient life kept in rock, such as shells, bones, leaves and footprints.

Era and period

Chapters of Earth's history. The four eras used here are the Precambrian, Palaeozoic, Mesozoic and Cenozoic and each era is split into periods such as the Jurassic.

Fossil record

One kind of fossil reported from one site in a scientific paper. A rich site can hold hundreds of records.

Part 1 · Discovery locations

Where are Australia's fossils found?

Fossil sites cover the whole continent, but they cluster where old rock reaches the surface and where people have searched longest: the south-east, the Flinders Ranges, the Kimberley and outback Queensland.

Hover over a circle or star for details. Use the menu under the map to show one era at a time.

Big states, few fossils per square kilometre

Queensland has the most fossil records, but it is also huge. Dividing by land area shows that Victoria and Tasmania are the most closely searched ground.

For scale, Peninsular Malaysia covers about 132,000 square kilometres, a little over half the size of Victoria.

The tiny Australian Capital Territory scores 1,429, the highest rate of all, because its land area is so small.

A few rock layers hold thousands of fossils

Some single rock formations have yielded over a thousand fossil records each. The Callytharra Formation in Western Australia, laid down in a Permian sea, tops the list.

Nine of the ten richest formations are from the Palaeozoic or Mesozoic, long before the dinosaurs died out.

Part 2 · Geological periods

How old are they?

Most of Australia's fossil records are older than the dinosaurs. Over half come from the Palaeozoic, when Australia was part of the supercontinent Gondwana and much of it lay under shallow seas.

Each state tells a different chapter

South Australia's typical fossil site is about 516 million years old, from the Cambrian, when animals with shells first appeared.

Victoria's typical site is about 112 million years old. Its fossils include dinosaurs from the southern coast and many younger sea shells.

Part 3 · Fossil types

What kinds of life do they show?

Fossils explain Earth's natural history by showing which life lived when. Australia's record moves from sea floors full of shelled animals to land covered by plants and roamed by backboned animals.

Sea first, land later

Lamp shells, sea animals with two shells, dominate the Permian with 4,665 records. They almost vanish after the Great Dying, the largest extinction in Earth's history.

The most recent period, the Quaternary, is rich in plant fossils and the bones of giant marsupials from caves such as Naracoorte.

Part 4 · Historical change

When were they discovered?

The earliest records in the database were described in the 1820s. Discovery stayed slow for over a century, then grew quickly after 1950. The 1990s and 2000s each added over 9,500 new fossil records.

No state stays on top

Victoria led in the 1950s and 1960s, New South Wales in the 1970s and Queensland in the 1980s. In the 2000s Western Australia took first place.

Since 1990 the top spot has changed hands almost every decade, so no single state holds the whole story.

Part 5 · Heritage preservation

Who looks after Australia's fossils?

Seven places on Australia's National Heritage List are protected for their fossils. These are marked with stars on the first map. Many of the specimens themselves have left the country, though.

Ediacara, Nilpena (South Australia)

Some of the oldest complex animals on Earth, about 550 million years old. Listed in 2007.

Riversleigh and Naracoorte (Queensland and South Australia)

The Australian Fossil Mammal Sites, a World Heritage property, record how Australia's mammals evolved. Listed in 2007.

Dinosaur Stampede, Lark Quarry (Queensland)

Thousands of dinosaur footprints about 95 million years old. Listed in 2004, the first of the seven.

West Kimberley, Willandra Lakes and Yea

Dinosaur tracks near Broome, ice age giant animals at Willandra and plants over 400 million years old at Yea. Listed in 2007 and 2011.

These counts cover only specimens tagged as fossils in the Global Biodiversity Information Facility, the largest global online catalogue of natural history collections. Australian museums keep large fossil collections of their own, but few are shared there as fossil specimens, so this shows what the world can see online, not everything Australia holds.

What it all means

Three things to take away

A record of deep time

From 3.4 billion year old stromatolites to ice age bones, Australia's rocks cover almost the whole story of life.

Discovery is still growing

Over 90% of fossil records were described after 1950 and the lead keeps passing from state to state.

Heritage held far away

Seven fossil places are protected at home, but only 4 in every 1,000 fossil specimens in the global online catalogue are held in Australia.

Notes on the data

Each fossil record is placed in the geological period that holds the middle of its age range. Fossil groups follow the scientific phylum: lamp shells are brachiopods and animals with backbones include fish, reptiles and mammals. Recent decades look smaller because new papers take time to enter the database.
